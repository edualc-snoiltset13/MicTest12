The Moon

Earth's only natural satellite and our closest celestial neighbor

Overview

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, orbiting our planet at an average distance of about 384,400 kilometers. It formed roughly 4.5 billion years ago, most likely from the debris of a giant impact between the early Earth and a Mars-sized body named Theia. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, so the same hemisphere — the near side — always faces us.

Phases of the Moon

As the Moon orbits Earth, the fraction of its sunlit half that we can see changes, producing the familiar cycle of phases. A complete cycle from one new moon to the next — a synodic month — takes about 29.5 days.

Surface and Geology

  • Maria: the dark plains visible from Earth are vast basalt flows from ancient volcanic eruptions.
  • Highlands: the bright, heavily cratered regions are the Moon's oldest crust.
  • Craters: with no atmosphere to burn up incoming rocks, impacts have scarred the surface for billions of years.
  • Regolith: the surface is blanketed in a layer of fine, glassy dust ground up by micrometeorite impacts.
  • Water ice: permanently shadowed craters near the poles hold deposits of frozen water.

Influence on Earth

The Moon's gravity raises Earth's ocean tides and helps stabilize the tilt of Earth's axis, which keeps our climate relatively steady over long timescales. Tidal interaction also slowly transfers energy from Earth to the Moon, so the Moon drifts away from us by about 3.8 centimeters every year, and Earth's days gradually lengthen.

Human Exploration

The Moon is the only world beyond Earth that humans have set foot on. The Soviet Luna 2 probe reached it in 1959, and NASA's Apollo 11 mission landed Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the Sea of Tranquility on July 20, 1969. Six Apollo missions brought back 382 kilograms of lunar rock between 1969 and 1972. Today, robotic missions from many nations continue to study the Moon, and NASA's Artemis program aims to return astronauts to its surface.
